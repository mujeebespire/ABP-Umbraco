ABPmer Marine and Coastal Survey undertakes bespoke coastal and inshore surveys around the UK and further afield.

About coastal adaptation and habitat creation

The shorelines and intertidal habitats of our coasts and estuaries provide valuable environmental, social and economic benefits.

However, they are under pressure across the world from human activities (past and present) and climate change. To respond to these pressures, many different shoreline adaptation and habitat creation projects have been carried out.

Such schemes are often designed to make coastlines more sustainable, maintain crucial wetlands and enhance marine biodiversity.

There are many reasons for carrying out shoreline adaptation work. The main two are

  1. to improve flood protection and address issues related to shoreline change and the effects of sea level rise;
  2. to restore/protect coastal habitats and offset either past, present or future losses of coastal habitat and biodiversity.

Many of the projects in this database have been pursued with these two central motives in mind. However there are other social and economic objectives associated with shoreline adaptation work. These wider benefits or 'Ecosystem Services' (i.e. the benefits people derive from nature and its restoration) are increasingly being recognised and valued.

A wide variety of techniques have been used, ranging from small schemes to vast landscape restorations.

Explore the database or read on to understand the different techniques.

Benefits of shoreline adaptation

There are many reasons for carrying out shoreline adaptation work. The main two are

  1. to improve flood protection and address issues related to shoreline change and the effects of sea level rise;
  2. to restore/protect coastal habitats and offset either past, present or future losses of coastal habitat and biodiversity.

Many of the projects in the OMReg database have been pursued with these two central motives in mind. However there are other social and economic objectives associated with shoreline adaptation work.

These wider benefits or 'Ecosystem Services' (i.e. the benefits people derive from nature and its restoration) are increasingly being recognised and valued.

Depending upon the scheme in question, these extra benefits can include any of the following:

  • Job creation during the project's implementation (short-term) or for the project's ongoing management
  • Provision of public space
  • Increased visitor numbers leading to economic gains for the region;
  • Visual enhancement of the 'seascape';
  • Public health and well-being benefits;
  • Enhancement of recreational and commercial fisheries;
  • Provision of food and raw materials;
  • Improvements to water quality;
  • Sequestration of carbon (especially in coastal wetlands);
  • Creation of resource for community involvement, engagement and education; and
  • A location for future research.
